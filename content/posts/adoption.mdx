## You Shipped the Package. Now the Real Work Begins

When you publish a shared utility, it feels like you’ve crossed the finish line.

You wrote the code.
You tested it.
You shipped it.
You posted the link in Slack or GitHub.

Nice work.

But here’s the uncomfortable truth: a shared tool that nobody uses isn’t an achievement. Adoption is the real goal.

And adoption is not a technical problem — it’s a human one.

## The Adoption Curve Is Real

![Adoption Curve Graph](/images/adoption-curve.jpg)

Most engineers have seen this curve before. But when you release a shared tool, it suddenly feels very real.

At first, only a few early adopters engage. They tolerate rough edges. They experiment. They give you feedback.

But the majority doesn’t move just because something exists. That’s where planning, patience, and persistence come in.

## Shipping Is Only Step One

I learned this while building a shared data utility for my team. We were proud of it — it solved real problems and reduced duplication. We expected immediate adoption.

It didn’t happen.

Only a few early adopters tried it. The majority hesitated. There was a learning curve, and adoption was slower than we expected.

That was the moment the bell curve became real for us. It forced us to ask: “Is partial adoption enough, or is broad adoption our real goal?”

We chose adoption. And that changed everything.

## Feedback Without Whiplash

Once people start using a tool, feedback starts coming in:

- “This doesn’t work for my use case.”
- “Can we add one more option?”
- “It needs to handle this edge case.”

It’s tempting to react immediately. But if every piece of feedback creates a new prop, the tool becomes bloated and confusing.

Every change must be evaluated holistically:

- Does it generalize?
- Does it align with the tool’s philosophy?
- Does it simplify or complicate the mental model?
- Are we solving a pattern or just patching an exception?

The goal isn’t to please everyone — it’s to maintain coherence.

## Take Off the Engineer Hat. Put On the Marketing Hat

Good ideas don’t sell themselves. Visibility, clarity, and repetition matter.

To drive adoption, you have to:

- Demo the tool consistently
- Share examples and documentation
- Repeat the same messages multiple times

Adoption doesn’t happen because someone heard about your tool once. It happens when:

- They have a real problem
- They remember the tool
- They trust it enough to try it

Repetition builds trust. Trust drives adoption.

## Make It Easier Than the Alternative

To move beyond early adopters, the tool has to be easier than what people are already using — not just technically better.

This means:

- Simplifying interfaces
- Improving naming
- Clarifying documentation
- Reducing onboarding friction

Every bit of friction removed widens the adoption curve. Eventually, adoption compounds and usage grows exponentially — just like the diagram predicts.

## Key Takeaways for Engineers

If you’re building something meant for others to use:

- Shipping isn’t enough. Plan for adoption.
- Feedback is critical — but evaluate it holistically.
- Communication and repetition are part of the job.
- Ease of use drives adoption faster than technical elegance.

Shared tools are leverage. But leverage only works if people actually use them.
